# ORISH’S WORLD @ THE CODE — POSITIVE BLACK HISTORY, INVENTORS, MAKERS & ACHIEVEMENTS

**Status:** Mandatory product/content pillar

This document supplements `docs/CODEX-HANDOFF-2026-08-23.md` and must be read together with it before major content/game work.

## Core principle

Orish’s World must include **positive Black history as an active, inspiring learning strand**, not as an occasional heritage page and not as content limited to slavery, racism or oppression.

Children should regularly discover Black inventors, scientists, engineers, mathematicians, doctors, explorers, builders, makers, artists, writers, entrepreneurs, community leaders and other people whose work, ideas and achievements changed everyday life, science, technology, culture and society.

The purpose is to give children accurate knowledge, pride, curiosity, possibility and a wider understanding of human achievement while remaining welcoming and educational for children of every background.

## Historical scope

Build a broad, age-appropriate view of Black history and achievement across different places and periods, including where suitable:

- Britain and Black British history
- Africa and African civilisations
- the Caribbean
- the United States
- Europe
- the wider African diaspora
- science, medicine and engineering
- mathematics and computing
- transport and infrastructure
- agriculture and food innovation
- invention and manufacturing
- space and astronomy
- literature and storytelling
- music, art, design and film
- sport and physical achievement
- entrepreneurship and business
- civic, community and social achievement

Do not imply that Black history begins with enslavement or that Black achievement is exceptional only because of adversity. Where difficult history is taught, it should be accurate and age appropriate, but it should sit inside a much broader story of people, cultures, ideas, innovation, family, creativity, leadership and accomplishment.

## Verification rule

Any real person, invention, date, quotation, scientific claim or historical event used in child-facing content must be **fact checked from reliable sources before publication**.

Do not repeat viral or simplified claims about an inventor merely because they appear frequently online. Clearly distinguish between someone who invented something, improved it, patented a version, pioneered its use, led a team, or made an important contribution.

When historians disagree, simplify carefully for the age group without pretending uncertainty does not exist.

## Make the history PLAYABLE

Do not turn this strand into a library of biographies children only read.

Create **positive games, missions, investigations, building challenges and cinema-to-game experiences inspired by real achievements**.

Examples of game structures:

### Inventor Workshop

A child enters a workshop inspired by a verified inventor or engineer. They inspect the problem that person was trying to solve, collect materials, test designs, identify why prototypes fail, improve the design and complete a working simulation.

The child learns **problem → idea → testing → improvement → impact**, rather than simply memorising a name and date.

### Who Made This Possible?

Children explore an everyday environment such as a city, home, hospital, transport hub, laboratory or communications room and discover contributions made by Black inventors, engineers, scientists and makers.

Correct answers unlock a short playable challenge connected to the real contribution.

### Build the Breakthrough

Children reconstruct an age-appropriate version of a historical innovation by dragging, assembling, sequencing, measuring or experimenting.

Older children should compare the historical technology with the modern version and investigate what changed.

### Hidden History Detective

Orish gives clues about a real person or achievement. Children search an environment, examine evidence, order a timeline, reject incorrect claims and identify the person or breakthrough.

This should teach research and evidence skills as well as history.

### Journey Through Time

A cinematic time-travel mission places Orish in a historically inspired setting. The child completes practical objectives connected to real life in that period, meets historically accurate characters or narrated figures where appropriate, and returns to the present to see the long-term impact.

Do not fabricate conversations or personal opinions and present them as quotations from real historical figures.

### Young Maker Challenge

After learning about an innovator, the child receives a modern problem and is challenged to invent their own solution using the same broad habits: observe, question, sketch, build, test, improve and explain.

This connects history to the child’s own creativity rather than presenting greatness as something that only happened in the past.

### Achievement Map

Create an explorable world/map where discoveries unlock people, inventions and achievements across countries, centuries and fields. It should visibly show that Black achievement is international and spans many disciplines.

### Cinema → Mission → Real Life

Where possible use the Orish’s World loop:

**WATCH → INVESTIGATE → PLAY → BUILD/TEST → DISCOVER → TRY SOMETHING IN REAL LIFE → RETURN TO STORY**

A short film about a breakthrough can lead directly into the relevant playable mission, then into a safe home experiment, drawing/building task or family discussion.

## Age adaptation

The same history strand should change with the child rather than disappearing at different ages.

- **Birth–3:** parent-led songs, faces, colours, objects, simple stories of people who made/build/helped/discovered things.
- **4–6:** simple hero stories, matching, building, sorting, sequencing and “what problem did they solve?” play.
- **7–10:** interactive biographies, invention/build missions, maps, timelines, science demonstrations and clue games.
- **11–13:** evidence investigations, source comparison, design challenges, historical context, myths-vs-evidence and deeper science/technology links.
- **14–16:** research missions, primary/secondary source evaluation, patents and innovation, business/economics, ethics, historical interpretation, overlooked contributors and independent projects.

Do not make older Black-history content feel like primary-school heritage material. It should mature into serious investigation, research, technology, economics, culture and critical thinking.

## Representation rule

Representation should be natural throughout Orish’s World, not confined to a single “Black History” area.

Black children and adults should appear naturally as:

- scientists
- engineers
- astronauts
- mathematicians
- coders
- doctors
- historians
- builders
- inventors
- artists
- researchers
- entrepreneurs
- teachers
- explorers
- leaders
- families

Girls and women must be represented naturally within these roles too.

The dedicated history strand can exist, but Black achievement should also be woven into science, maths, cinema, money, civics, maker activities, space, literacy and general missions.

## Tone

The tone should be exciting, truthful and empowering without becoming preachy or presenting any race as superior to another.

The child should leave thinking:

- “I didn’t know that.”
- “That was clever.”
- “How did they work that out?”
- “I want to try building something.”
- “People like me can do this too.”
- “I wonder what else history has missed.”
