# Orish's World — Gameplay Excitement Standard

## Purpose

Orish's World must feel like a living adventure, not a worksheet with animation.

A learning objective is not enough on its own. Each major game needs a strong player fantasy, meaningful action, changing context and a satisfying discovery loop.

## Non-negotiable rule

A major game should not rely mainly on:

- repeated multiple-choice cards
- repeating one gesture until a meter fills
- passive reading between button presses
- points/stars as the only reason to continue
- decorative movement that does not change the problem

Those interactions can appear inside a game, but they cannot be the whole game.

## Core loop

Every major mission should aim to include at least five of these seven elements:

1. **Mission** — a clear reason to act now.
2. **Movement / manipulation** — the player physically explores, moves, builds, scans, sorts, routes, aims, times or operates something.
3. **Investigation** — clues must be found, compared or tested.
4. **Changing situation** — new information, a route change, a countdown, a system fault, a surprise discovery or a consequence changes what the player should do.
5. **Meaningful choice** — choices affect route, evidence, resources, ending or later difficulty.
6. **Discovery / reveal** — hidden areas, unexpected evidence, a new location, a cinematic reveal or a mystery answer.
7. **Payoff** — the result is shown in the world, not only as a score.

## Age adaptation

### Birth–3
Parent-led sensory cause-and-effect, rhythm, movement and shared discovery. No pressure mechanics.

### Ages 4–6
Short playful missions, movement, finding, matching, building, sound, surprise reveals and immediate feedback. Mistakes should guide, not punish.

### Ages 7–10
Exploration, simple strategy, environmental puzzles, collecting evidence, rescue/build missions, hidden routes and multi-step discoveries.

### Ages 11–13
Investigation, contradictions, evidence chains, time pressure used sparingly, route planning, system puzzles, stealth-like observation without violence, consequences and replayable outcomes.

### Ages 14–16
Systems thinking, strategy, trade-offs, research, branching outcomes, resource pressure, live information changes, complex evidence and decisions that visibly alter the world.

## Tension without violence

Excitement can come from:

- a signal disappearing
- a storm approaching a dig site
- power failing in a lab
- a rover losing contact
- a route closing
- a rescue window narrowing
- a habitat system becoming unstable
- conflicting evidence arriving
- a hidden chamber opening
- an experiment behaving unexpectedly
- a budget shock
- a mystery changing after a new clue
- a time-limited optional bonus objective

Never use fear, humiliation or loss of earned learning progress as punishment.

## Learning integration

Learning should be required to act effectively in the world.

Bad pattern:

> Answer a maths question to open an unrelated door.

Better pattern:

> Calculate the correct power split because the observatory needs enough energy for the scanner and life-support system at the same time.

Bad pattern:

> Read a fact card after finding a fossil.

Better pattern:

> Use tooth shape, bone structure and plant evidence to decide how to reconstruct the animal and where to search next.

## Reward rule

Stars, badges and streaks support the experience; they are not the experience.

Primary rewards should include:

- new area unlocked
- story progression
- changed environment
- new evidence
- new ability/tool
- cinematic reveal
- alternate ending
- stronger Learning Passport evidence
- optional real-world mission

## Mistakes

A wrong action should normally produce one of:

- a useful clue
- a smaller next choice
- a demonstration
- a changed route
- a consequence the player can repair

Do not rely on repeated "wrong / try again" loops.

## Replayability

At least some major missions should vary by:

- clue placement
- route
- optional objectives
- evidence order
- world event
- resource pressure
- final outcome

## The Orish rule

Orish should not constantly explain the game.

Orish should act like a guide inside the world:

- brief before mission
- react to discoveries
- ask an occasional useful question
- warn when the situation changes
- celebrate genuine reasoning
- debrief afterwards

The player should spend more time doing than listening.

## Current priority upgrades

### Life City
Move beyond fixed scan-and-multiple-choice structure. Add playable evidence verification, changing city conditions, resource manipulation and branching visible outcomes.

### Space Signal
Keep movement, but deepen navigation, scanner use, environmental systems, signal behaviour, hidden routes and multi-stage evidence. Mini-games must affect the mission rather than interrupt it.

### Fossil Detective
Replace repeated scan/brush/read cycle with an open dig strategy: choose dig zones, interpret partial clues, protect fragile finds, reconstruct the animal, respond to weather/site changes and unlock a hidden final discovery.

## Release gate

Before calling a major game "finished", ask:

- Would a child want to replay this if there were no stars?
- Does the player do more than choose answers?
- Does the world change because of the player's actions?
- Is there a moment of surprise or discovery?
- Does the learning help solve the mission itself?
- Is there a meaningful decision or strategy?
- Is the experience appropriate for its age band?

If most answers are no, the game is still an activity prototype, not a finished Orish's World game.
